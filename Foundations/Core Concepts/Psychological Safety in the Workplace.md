
# **Psychological Safety in the Workplace: Systemic Risks, Governance Obligations, and Ethical Imperatives**

- <Kristopher.Hewett@sa.gov.au>
- Revised: 2025-12-02

***

## **Abstract**

Psychological safety is now recognised as a legal and ethical cornerstone of workplace health and safety in Australia. Despite this, systemic governance failures, inequitable practices, and rhetorical minimisation continue to undermine compliance and cultural integrity. This paper critically examines psychosocial hazards through the lens of **Safe Work Australia’s Model Code of Practice (2022, 2024)**, **Commonwealth WHS Regulations**, and **ISO 45003:2021**, alongside ethical theories of harm. It argues that harm minimisation strategies-common in organisational responses-are insufficient and perpetuate impunity, eroding trust and social cohesion.

***

## **Introduction**

Under Australia’s harmonised **Work Health and Safety (WHS) laws**, employers and Persons Conducting a Business or Undertaking (PCBUs) have a **positive duty** to eliminate or minimise psychosocial risks so far as reasonably practicable (Safe Work Australia, 2022; Comcare, 2024). Psychological safety refers to an environment where individuals feel secure to express ideas, raise concerns, and participate without fear of humiliation or retaliation.

Recent reforms-including the **Commonwealth Code of Practice: Managing Psychosocial Hazards at Work (2024)** and state-based regulations-signal a decisive shift from voluntary well-being initiatives to enforceable governance obligations (Safe Work Australia, 2022; HFW, 2024).

***

## **Psychosocial Hazards and Their Impact**

Safe Work Australia identifies **17 categories of psychosocial hazards**, including excessive job demands, poor organisational change management, inadequate support, bullying, harassment, and poor organisational justice (Safe Work Australia, 2022). These hazards interact and compound, creating systemic risks that undermine psychological health and organisational performance.

### **1. Poor Organisational Change Management**

Failure to engage staff in cultural and inclusion initiatives-such as symbolic diversity actions-signals performative inclusion. This inconsistency erodes trust and belonging, reinforcing systemic inequities.
**Regulatory Context:** Poor change management is explicitly listed as a psychosocial hazard under the Model Code of Practice (Safe Work Australia, 2022).
**Ethical Lens:** Reactive harm minimisation dominates organisational responses, while proactive harm avoidance-systemic prevention-is neglected.

***

### **2. Poor Organisational Justice**

Inconsistent handling of complaints and selective responsiveness perpetuate perceptions of inequity.
**Governance Dimension:** The **Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth)** now imposes a **positive duty** on employers to proactively prevent discrimination and harassment, shifting from reactive compliance to active prevention (AHRC, 2023).

***

### **3. Conflict and Poor Workplace Relationships**

Persistent undermining behaviours-interruptions, dismissiveness-create microaggressions that erode psychological safety.
**Regulatory Context:** Poor interpersonal relationships are recognised as psychosocial hazards under WHS laws and ISO 45003 (Safe Work Australia, 2022; ISO, 2021).

***

### **4. Harassment and Discrimination**

Observed discriminatory behaviours-even as a bystander-constitute psychosocial harm.
**Legal Framework:** Under WHS laws and anti-discrimination legislation, PCBUs must eliminate or minimise these risks. The **Commonwealth Code of Practice (2024)** reinforces this obligation, requiring application of the **hierarchy of controls** (Comcare, 2024).

***

## **Ethics of Harm: Avoidance vs Minimisation**

Organisations frequently adopt harm minimisation strategies-reactive, compliance-driven measures-rather than harm avoidance, which demands proactive elimination of systemic risks. This ethical gap sustains psychosocial hazards and undermines psychological safety (Author, 2023).

***

## **Governance and Impunity**

Despite robust frameworks, enforcement gaps and discretionary compliance perpetuate impunity. The **Boland Review (2018)** and subsequent reforms emphasise that psychosocial risk management is now a **core compliance expectation**, not a discretionary well-being initiative (Safe Work Australia, 2022; AICD, 2025).

***

## **Recommendations**

1. **Embed Harm Avoidance:** Shift from reactive harm minimisation to proactive elimination of psychosocial hazards.
2. **Strengthen Accountability:** Close procedural gaps enabling selective enforcement; apply **positive duty** principles under the Sex Discrimination Act.
3. **Integrate ISO 45003 and AS/NZS ISO 45003:** Align governance frameworks with international best practice.
4. **Transparent Communication:** Publish progress on inclusion and psychological safety initiatives.
5. **Training and Awareness:** Address systemic bias, rhetorical minimisation, and governance ethics through mandatory programs.

***

## **Conclusion**

Psychological safety cannot be achieved through symbolic gestures or reactive compliance. It requires systemic reform, ethical clarity, and governance accountability. Organisations must embrace harm avoidance as a guiding principle, dismantle structures that perpetuate impunity, and foster environments where equity and inclusion are non-negotiable.
